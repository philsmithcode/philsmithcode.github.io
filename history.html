A History of Computer Science at Birmingham

From the appointment of Birmingham's first Lecturer in Computing in 1959, through mainframes, minicomputers, artificial intelligence, cyber security and data science, to a School whose teaching and research now span the full breadth of modern computing.

Computing at Birmingham is almost as old as the discipline itself.

The institutional story begins in 1959, when a Lectureship in Computing was created in the Department of Mathematical Physics. What began as an effort to provide scarce computing capacity for researchers across the University gradually developed into a distinct academic discipline, a suite of degree programmes, and ultimately the School of Computer Science.

The history is also a story of changing technology. Birmingham moved from paper tape, punched cards and a room-sized KDF9, through interactive terminals, UNIX minicomputers and Multics, to high-performance computing, artificial intelligence, cyber security, robotics, data science and today's increasingly AI-rich curriculum.

About this page. The chronology to 2006 is a condensed and rewritten version of the School's former history website, preserved by the Internet Archive. The later section extends that record using public University of Birmingham sources. This updated edition also integrates openly reusable modern photographs of the School and its surroundings from 2006 onwards.

1959–1969 · From computing service to academic discipline

The KDF9 years
1959

The first Lectureship in Computing

The University created a post of Lecturer in Computing within Mathematical Physics and appointed Ken Redish. At roughly the same time, Birmingham began planning a University-wide computing service that would support research in many disciplines while also creating space for research into computing itself.

1961–1965

The KDF9 arrives — eventually

Birmingham placed a provisional order in January 1961 for an English Electric KDF9. The project proved more difficult than expected: delivery and configuration became the subject of a prolonged disagreement with the manufacturer. A strengthened 16K-word configuration finally passed acceptance testing on 21 June 1965.

The machine occupied seven large cabinets. Rather than being installed in the Watson Building, it was housed beneath the Great Hall in the lower ground floor of Aston Webb, in space previously used as a student cloakroom. Programs were prepared using paper tape and teletypes.

1965–1967

A University Computing Service becomes the Computer Centre

With the KDF9 accepted, the University Computing Service became fully operational. Computing lecturers taught programming to staff and research students, contributed optional courses to Mathematical Sciences degrees, developed software and pursued research, initially with a strong numerical-analysis emphasis.

In 1966 the KDF9 received a major upgrade, adding disk storage, punched-card input/output, a high-speed printer and improved online access. In 1967 the unit was renamed the Computer Centre; Stuart Hollingdale succeeded Ken Redish as Director.

1969

Computer Science becomes a degree subject

The Computer Centre was granted academic-department status within the Faculty of Science and Engineering for the purpose of running Computer Science degrees. The MSc in Computer Science admitted its first students in October 1969.

This sharpened a distinction that would shape the next two decades: one part of the Centre provided computing services to the University, while another undertook academic teaching and research. The Centre was consequently organised into Service and Academic divisions.

1970s · Degrees, dedicated machines and interactive computing

Building an academic department
1972

A new mainframe and Birmingham's first undergraduate Computer Science route

The KDF9 was retired and replaced by an ICL 1906A in a purpose-built Elms Road facility. The Service Division moved with it, while the Academic Division remained around Aston Webb. The resulting physical separation accelerated the emergence of Computer Science as a conventional academic department.

The same year saw the first intake to the BSc in Mathematics and Computer Science, initially one route within the School of Mathematical Sciences.

1973

The first computer bought solely for Computer Science

A Modular One minicomputer was purchased for research and specialist teaching. Unlike the University's central machines, it existed specifically for Computer Science, supporting work in programming languages, operating systems and MSc projects. BCPL was a principal language used on the system.

1976–1977

The Centre for Computing and Computer Science

In 1976 the Computer Centre became the Centre for Computing and Computer Science. The period also exposed how dependent teaching still was on central computing infrastructure: a long operators' strike in 1976–77 took the ICL 1906A out of service and badly disrupted practical programming. A Prime minicomputer was temporarily installed in the old KDF9 room so teaching could continue.

In 1977 the BSc in Computer Science and Electronic Engineering admitted its first students.

1978–1979

Online terminals and UNIX

A DEC2050 (commonly called the DEC20) brought genuinely interactive computing to much larger numbers of students. Instead of submitting punched-card jobs and waiting for results, students could edit and run programs from online terminals. Demand grew rapidly and the system was soon upgraded.

In 1979 a PDP-11/34 running UNIX was installed in the Academic Division for research and teaching, replacing the Modular One.

1980s · Software Engineering, microcomputers and Multics

A broader computing curriculum
1980

Software Engineering enters the degree portfolio

The BSc in Computer Science/Software Engineering took its first students. The archived School history records it as the first UK undergraduate degree course in Software Engineering. In the same year, the University established a Microprocessor Laboratory split between Computer Science and Electronic and Electrical Engineering, reflecting the growing importance of smaller programmable systems.

1983

Multics becomes the University's main computing system

A Honeywell Multics system replaced the 1906A as Birmingham's principal University computer. It supported terminals distributed across campus, including a terminal room in Aston Webb used heavily by Computer Science students. The shift illustrates how quickly interactive computing had moved from novelty to an expected part of teaching and research.

1990s · The School of Computer Science

A School in its own right
1990

The School is established

The Centre for Computing and Computer Science was divided, with its Academic Division becoming the School of Computer Science in the Faculty of Science. The archived history describes the 1990s as a period of exceptional growth in research, teaching, student numbers and staff.

1992–1994

New combinations of computing, engineering and AI

The BEng/MEng in Electronic and Software Engineering began in 1992, replacing the older Computer Science and Electronic Engineering degree. In 1993, Birmingham added BSc programmes in Artificial Intelligence and Computer Science and in Psychology and Artificial Intelligence. A Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence BSc followed in 1994.

These programmes show that Artificial Intelligence was embedded in Birmingham's teaching portfolio decades before today's generative-AI boom.

2000s · A new building and a wider postgraduate portfolio

West Campus and programme expansion
2000

A permanent home on West Campus

The School moved out of Aston Webb into the Computer Science building as part of the University's West Campus redevelopment, giving the discipline a purpose-built base after decades spread across older University spaces.

2001

A major curriculum refresh

The undergraduate portfolio was substantially revised. The older Computer Science/Software Engineering BSc was replaced by BEng and MEng routes, while a new BSc in Computer Science took its first students. At postgraduate level, MSc Natural Computation joined the School's portfolio.

The School also installed what its archived history described as the UK's first Itanium2-based 64-bit cluster, reflecting the increasing importance of high-performance computing infrastructure.

2004–2006

New interdisciplinary and specialist Masters routes

In 2004 the Computer Science/Software Engineering BSc gave way to Computer Science with Business Management, while the MSc Internet Software Systems began. By 2006 the School had expanded to six MSc programmes, with Computer Security and Intelligent Systems Engineering among the additions.

2010s · Cyber security, industry partnerships and an international School

Research strength and new models of education
2013–2017

National recognition for cyber-security research

Birmingham's established security research became formally recognised through the UK's Academic Centre of Excellence in Cyber Security Research (ACE-CSR) scheme. A first documented funding period began at the end of 2013, and the University was re-accredited by the National Cyber Security Centre and EPSRC in 2017 for a further five years.

By this point, cyber security and privacy had become one of the School's most visible areas of external impact, with work spanning applied cryptography, transport systems, electronic voting, infrastructure, hardware and connected devices.

2014

Strong national research assessment

In REF 2014, the School was reported within the top quartile of UK Computer Science institutions. Around the same period the School's public profile increasingly highlighted robotics, natural computation, imaging, human-computer interaction, theory and security alongside its long-standing AI activity.

2017–2018

Degree apprenticeships and Dubai

In 2017 the University and PwC announced a four-year, fully funded technology degree apprenticeship built around a Birmingham Computer Science degree. The first cohort arrived in 2018, combining academic study with work-based technology projects.

Computer Science was also among the first subjects offered at the University of Birmingham Dubai. The Dubai campus opened in 2018 and welcomed its first students that September, extending Birmingham Computer Science teaching beyond the Edgbaston campus.

2018–2019

Data science, AI and continued growth

The University became a partner of The Alan Turing Institute in 2018, deepening links to the UK's national institute for data science and AI. By 2019 the School was reporting substantial recruitment of new academic staff in cyber security, artificial intelligence and theoretical computer science, alongside its largest-ever first-year undergraduate cohort at the time.

2020–present · AI, data science and a larger, more interdisciplinary School

The contemporary School
2020

New Masters programmes — and teaching through a pandemic

The School launched new advanced MSc programmes in Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning and in Data Science. The year also brought the COVID-19 closure of the physical building and a rapid move to online teaching and collaboration; students completed their degrees remotely and the School held its first virtual graduation celebration.

2021

Interdisciplinary Data Science and AI

The postgraduate portfolio expanded again with Responsible Data Science, developed jointly with the School of Law, and FinTech in partnership with Birmingham Business School. In September, the University formally launched the Institute for Interdisciplinary Data Science and Artificial Intelligence, drawing together expertise across the institution with Computer Science playing a central role.

REF 2021

Third in the UK for research GPA and 4* research

The University's published REF 2021 results place Computer Science 3rd in the UK for research GPA and for 4* research. The current School describes its work through themes that include Science for AI & AI for Science, Theory of Computation, Cyber Security and Privacy, Socio-Technical Systems, and Perception, Language and Action.

2022–2026

Scale, international reach and continuing curriculum evolution

The first PwC technology degree-apprenticeship cohort graduated in 2022. Computer Science continues to be taught at both Edgbaston and Dubai, while the School's undergraduate and postgraduate portfolios have continued to evolve around contemporary computing practice, AI, software engineering, cyber security, data science and research-led specialist study.

In the 2026 National Student Survey, Artificial Intelligence & Computer Science was one of sixteen Birmingham subject areas reported as first in the Russell Group. The School's current public description remains close to the ambition visible throughout this history: to combine fundamental Computer Science with applied work capable of changing other disciplines, industry and society.

Research today

The School's current research structure spans AI, theory, cyber security and privacy, socio-technical systems, computer vision and imaging, natural language processing, robotics, cognitive science and interdisciplinary applications.

Teaching today

Undergraduate, integrated Masters and postgraduate programmes combine core Computer Science with areas such as AI, security, software engineering and data science, alongside industry links, international delivery and research-led specialist options.
